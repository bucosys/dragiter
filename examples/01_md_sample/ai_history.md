# The Dawn of Machine Thinking: Early Attempts in AI

Before the first line of code was ever written for a neural network, the idea of an artificial mind existed in the realm of philosophy and fiction. This document explores the foundational years of Artificial Intelligence, from conceptual dreams to the first functional programs.

## 1. The Philosophical Foundations and Turing's Dream

The quest for artificial intelligence did not begin with computers, but with the question of whether human thought could be reduced to a mechanical process. In the 1940s and 50s, pioneers like Alan Turing began to formalize this inquiry. Turing’s seminal 1950 paper, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," bypassed the difficult definition of "thinking" and replaced it with a practical test: The Imitation Game.

Turing argued that if a machine could converse with a human such that the human could not distinguish it from another person, the machine should be considered intelligent. This period was characterized by "Symbolic AI" aspirations—the belief that intelligence is essentially the manipulation of symbols according to logical rules. Early researchers were optimistic, believing that the "problem of AI" could be solved within a generation if enough logic gates were correctly aligned.

## 2. The Dartmouth Workshop of 1956

The official birth of AI as a field of study is widely cited as the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence in 1956. Organized by John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon, the workshop brought together the brightest minds of the era.

It was here that McCarthy coined the term "Artificial Intelligence." The participants explored themes that still dominate the field today: language processing, neuron nets, complexity theory, and self-improvement. While the workshop did not produce an immediate breakthrough, it established a community. The researchers left Dartmouth with a profound sense of optimism, famously predicting that a significant advance in making machines use language and form abstractions could be made in a single summer.

## 3. Logic Theorist and the First Programs

During the late 1950s, Allen Newell and Herbert A. Simon developed the "Logic Theorist," often considered the first true AI program. It was designed to mimic the problem-solving skills of a human and successfully proved 38 of the first 52 theorems in Whitehead and Russell's *Principia Mathematica*.

Shortly after, the "General Problem Solver" (GPS) was introduced. Unlike the Logic Theorist, GPS was intended to solve any formalized symbolic problem. This era saw the rise of the "search and planning" paradigm. Researchers believed that intelligence was simply a matter of searching through a "state space" of possibilities to find a goal. However, they soon encountered the "combinatorial explosion"—as problems became slightly more complex, the number of possible moves grew beyond the capacity of any existing computer.

## 4. ELIZA and the Illusion of Understanding

In the mid-1960s, Joseph Weizenbaum at MIT created ELIZA, a program that could simulate a conversation with a psychotherapist. ELIZA operated on a surprisingly simple mechanism: it looked for keywords in the user's input and applied transformation rules to generate a response (e.g., if a user said "My mother," ELIZA might reply "Tell me more about your family").

Despite its simplicity, ELIZA had a profound psychological impact. Users often attributed deep understanding and empathy to the program, a phenomenon now known as the "ELIZA Effect." While ELIZA did not "understand" a single word it processed, it demonstrated that human-like interaction could be mimicked through clever pattern matching, raising early ethical questions about the nature of machine-human relationships.

## 5. The First AI Winter and the Limits of Scaling

By the early 1970s, the initial euphoria began to fade. The ambitious promises made in the 1950s had not materialized. Computers lacked the memory and processing power to handle real-world data, and the symbolic approach struggled with the "common sense" knowledge that humans take for granted.

In 1973, the Lighthill Report in the UK and a similar withdrawal of funding by DARPA in the US led to the first "AI Winter." Critics argued that AI research had reached a dead end. However, this period of reduced funding was not a total loss; it forced researchers to refine their methods, eventually leading to the development of expert systems in the 1980s and, much later, the connectionist revolution that powers today's Large Language Models.
